## Topic G1A. General class control operator frequency privileges; primary and secondary allocations

Where a General may transmit on HF. Generals get all of 160, 60, 30, 17, 12 and 10 meters, and most of 80, 40, 20 and 15 meters. On those four bands, some segments are reserved for Amateur Extra licensees.

- General segments on 80/75 m: 3.525–3.600 and 3.800–4.000 MHz. ([47 CFR §97.301](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.301))
- On 40 m: 7.025–7.125 and 7.175–7.300 MHz (7.125–7.175 is Extra/Advanced only). ([47 CFR §97.301](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.301))
- On 20 m: 14.025–14.150 and 14.225–14.350 MHz. ([47 CFR §97.301](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.301))
- On 15 m: 21.025–21.200 and 21.275–21.450 MHz. ([47 CFR §97.301](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.301))
- 30 meters is CW, RTTY and data only: no phone or image. ([47 CFR §97.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.305))
- CW is allowed anywhere you have privileges. ([47 CFR §97.305](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.305))
- Secondary users must not interfere with, and must accept interference from, primary users. ([47 CFR §97.303](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.303))
- On 10 meters, repeaters are allowed only above 29.5 MHz. ([47 CFR §97.205](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.205))

- Diagram: General class HF privileges in the US (ITU Region 2). Where Generals don't get a whole voice segment, they get its upper part.

## Topic G1B. Antenna structure limitations; good engineering and good amateur practice; beacon operation; prohibited transmissions; retransmitting radio signals

Antenna structures, beacons, permitted one-way transmissions and retransmissions, and the catch-all of good engineering and good amateur practice.

- Antenna structures over 200 feet, or near a public-use airport, need FAA notification and FCC registration. ([47 CFR §97.15](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.15))
- PRB-1: state and local rules must reasonably accommodate amateur radio and be the minimum practicable regulation. ([47 CFR §97.15](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.15))
- Beacons are for observing propagation and reception. ([47 CFR §97.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.3))
- Beacon power is limited to 100 W. ([47 CFR §97.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.203))
- A beacon may use only one channel per band from one location. ([47 CFR §97.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.203))
- On HF, beacons may run under automatic control only on 28.20–28.30 MHz. ([47 CFR §97.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.203))
- Any station may occasionally retransmit US government weather and propagation forecasts. ([47 CFR §97.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.113))
- Abbreviations and Q signals are fine; codes meant to obscure meaning are not. ([47 CFR §97.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.113))
- Anything not covered by the rules must follow good engineering and good amateur practice. ([47 CFR §97.101](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.101))

## Topic G1C. Transmitter power regulations; data emission standards; 60-meter operation requirements

Power limits, the special 60-meter rules, and digital codes.

- The general limit is 1500 W PEP output. ([47 CFR §97.313](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.313))
- 30 meters (10.10–10.15 MHz) is limited to 200 W PEP. ([47 CFR §97.313](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.313))
- Power is measured as PEP: the average power during one RF cycle at the peak of the modulation. ([47 CFR §97.3](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.3))
- On 60 m, emissions must be no wider than 2.8 kHz. ([47 CFR §97.303](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.303))
- 60 m power is limited in ERP relative to a dipole, so keep antenna gain records if you use something else. ([47 CFR §97.313](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.313))
- Data techniques may be used if their technical characteristics are publicly documented. ([47 CFR §97.309](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.309))

## Topic G2A. Phone operating procedures: USB/LSB conventions, breaking into a contact, transmitter setup for voice operation; answering DX stations

Phone operating on HF. Single sideband is the main voice mode: lower sideband below 10 MHz and upper sideband above, by convention rather than rule. Also covered: joining a conversation, VOX, ALC and answering DX calls.

- SSB sends only one sideband; the carrier and the other sideband are suppressed. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Dropping the carrier and one sideband saves both power and bandwidth compared with AM. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Convention: LSB below 10 MHz (160, 75, 40 m), USB above (20 m and up, VHF/UHF). 60 m is the exception and uses USB. ([IARU Region 2 Band Plan (September 2020)](https://www.iaru.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/IARU-Region-2-Band-plan.pdf))
- To join a conversation, say your call sign once between transmissions. "Break" is for emergencies. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/making-your-first-contact))
- VOX switches to transmit when you speak, for hands-free operation. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/making-your-first-contact))
- If the ALC reading is too high, turn down the microphone gain. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/making-your-first-contact))

## Topic G2B. Operating effectively; band plans; drills and emergencies; RACES operation

Sharing the bands: nobody owns a frequency, emergencies come first, how far apart to stay from other signals, voluntary band plans, nets and RACES.

- No frequency is assigned for any station's exclusive use; stations must cooperate. ([47 CFR §97.101](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.101))
- Emergency communications always get priority. ([47 CFR §97.101](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.101))
- An SSB voice signal is about 2.7 kHz wide, so stay 2–3 kHz from other SSB stations. ([47 CFR §2.202](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-2.202))
- CW signals are much narrower (band plans allow 200 Hz), so 150–500 Hz of separation is enough. ([IARU Region 2 Band Plan (September 2020)](https://www.iaru.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/IARU-Region-2-Band-plan.pdf))
- Before calling CQ, ask whether the frequency is in use ("QRL?" on CW). ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/making-your-first-contact))
- Band plans are voluntary, but following them is good amateur practice. ([IARU Region 2 Band Plan (September 2020)](https://www.iaru.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/IARU-Region-2-Band-plan.pdf))
- 50.100–50.125 MHz is the 6-meter DX window, for contacts with stations outside the 48 contiguous states. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/band-plan))
- A RACES control operator must hold an FCC amateur license and be enrolled with a civil defense organization. ([47 CFR §97.407](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.407))
- RACES drills and tests are limited to 1 hour per week. ([47 CFR §97.407](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/section-97.407))

## Topic G2C. CW operating procedures and procedural signals; Q signals; full break-in

CW operating: full break-in, Q signals and prosigns, matching speed and frequency, and RST reports. A Q signal followed by a question mark asks the question; without it, it gives the answer.

- QSK (full break-in): you hear the other station between your own dots and dashes. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- QRS: send more slowly. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- QRL: I am busy. QRL? asks if the frequency is in use. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- QSL: I acknowledge receipt. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- QRN: I am troubled by static. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- QRV: I am ready. ([Recommendation ITU-R M.1172](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1172/en))
- KN: only the station you're working should answer. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/quick-reference-operating-aids))
- AR: end of message. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/files/file/Public%20Service/fsd218.pdf))
- RST suffixes: C for chirp, K for key clicks, X for crystal-steady. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/quick-reference-operating-aids))
- Zero beat the station you answer, and don't send faster than it does. ([ARRL](https://www.arrl.org/making-your-first-contact))

## Topic G3A. Sunspots and solar radiation; geomagnetic field and stability indices

The Sun drives HF propagation. Its ultraviolet and X-rays ionize the upper atmosphere, and that output rises and falls over the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle. Flares, coronal mass ejections and coronal holes can disrupt conditions, and the K and A indices rate how disturbed Earth's magnetic field is.

- Sunspot numbers and the Sun's ultraviolet output rise and fall together; both track the F10.7 index. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/f107-cm-radio-emissions))
- Solar EUV and X-ray output varies about tenfold over the 11-year cycle, and the ionosphere's density follows it. A denser ionosphere supports higher usable frequencies. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/ionosphere))
- The solar flux index is the Sun's radio output at 10.7 cm (2800 MHz). ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/f107-cm-radio-emissions))
- A flare's X-rays arrive at the speed of light (about 8 minutes) and boost D-region absorption on the daylit side: a sudden ionospheric disturbance. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/solar-flares-radio-blackouts))
- Coronal mass ejections take 15 hours to several days to arrive. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/coronal-mass-ejections))
- Coronal holes send high-speed solar wind that can trigger geomagnetic storms, and can recur with the Sun's 27-day rotation. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/coronal-holes))
- Auroras can reflect VHF signals far beyond normal range. ([G. Lange-Hesse](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19650029499))
- Geomagnetic storms disrupt HF, especially at high latitudes. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/impacts/hf-radio-communications))
- K-index: geomagnetic disturbance over 3 hours, 0 to 9. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/products/station-k-and-indices))
- A-index: the day's average, a longer-term measure. ([NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/products/station-k-and-indices))

### G3A01

- [NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, Ionosphere](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/ionosphere): “The amount of energy (photon flux) at EUV and x-ray wavelengths varies by nearly a factor of ten over the 11 year solar cycle. The density of the ionosphere changes accordingly.”
- [NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, F10.7 cm radio emissions](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/f107-cm-radio-emissions): “The F10.7 correlates well with the sunspot number as well as a number of UltraViolet (UV) and visible solar irradiance records.”

## Topic G3B. Maximum Usable Frequency; Lowest Usable Frequency; short path and long path propagation; determining propagation conditions; ionospheric refraction

For any path at any moment there is a window of usable frequencies. Above the maximum usable frequency (MUF), signals pass through the ionosphere. Below the lowest usable frequency (LUF), they are absorbed. The best signals are just below the MUF. One hop off the E region covers up to about 1,200 miles; off the higher F2 region, about 2,500 miles.

- MUF: the maximum usable frequency between two points. Higher frequencies are refracted less. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- LUF: below it, absorption makes the signal too weak to receive. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- The F2 layer, and so the MUF, varies with time of day, season and solar activity. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- One E-region hop reaches about 1,200 miles. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Atmospheric noise is worse at lower frequencies, which is why summer static is heavy on the low bands. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))

- Diagram: Between the LUF and the MUF, the ionosphere returns signals to Earth. If the LUF rises above the MUF, ordinary skywave can't cover the path.

## Topic G3C. Ionospheric regions; critical angle and frequency; HF scatter; near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS)

The ionosphere's regions, from lowest to highest, are D, E, F1 and F2. The D region absorbs; the F2 region carries the longest skip. Also covered: critical frequency and angle, the skip zone and scatter, and NVIS.

- The D region is the lowest region of the ionosphere. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- In daytime the D region absorbs HF (worst on the lower bands). It disappears at night. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- F2 is the main layer for long-distance HF. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- The F layer is the highest (about 90 to 240 miles up), so it gives the longest hops. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Critical frequency: the highest frequency a layer returns to Earth (for a wave sent straight up). ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Critical angle: waves leaving at steeper angles go through the layer and are lost. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))
- Skip zone: the silent gap between ground-wave range and the first skywave landing. Weak, fluttery scatter signals can sometimes be heard there. ([US Navy](https://archive.org/details/NEETSModules))

- Diagram: Waves sent above the critical angle are lost. Between the ground wave and the first skywave landing is the skip zone. NVIS uses a low enough frequency that near-vertical waves return close to home.
